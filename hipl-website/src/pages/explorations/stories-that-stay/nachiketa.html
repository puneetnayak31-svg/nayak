Nachiketa and the King of Death

A teenager is sent to Death, and refuses every reward Death offers, until he gets an answer.

Scene 1

The father's gift

Vājaśravas is performing a great sacrifice in which he is supposed to give away what he owns. But the cows he hands over as gifts are old and worn out, past giving milk. His young son, Nachiketa, watches, and questions whether gifts like these can do any good.

Scene 2

"To whom will you give me?"

If his father is giving everything away, Nachiketa reasons, then he too must be given to someone. He asks, and asks again. Finally his father snaps in anger: "To Death I give you."

Pause and think

Was Nachiketa being disrespectful, or honest?

Scene 3

Three nights at Death's door

Nachiketa goes to the house of Yama. Death is away. The boy waits at the door for three nights without food. When Yama returns, he is troubled that a guest has been kept waiting, and offers three boons, one for each night, to make amends.

Scene 4

Three boons

Nachiketa asks for three things: that his father be at peace and welcome him home; to learn a sacred fire ritual; and to know what happens after death. Which would you ask first?

Choose your question

Granted at once

Yama agrees immediately: the father will recognise his son, sleep peacefully and lose his anger. Nachiketa's first thought is for the relationship he left behind.

Taught, and named after him

Yama teaches the fire ritual, and is so pleased with how quickly the boy learns that the fire is named after Nachiketa. VERIFY VERSE

Refused, then tested

Here Death hesitates. Even the gods have doubted this, he says; ask for something else. The hardest question is the one the story is really about.

Scene 5

The temptation

Instead of the answer, Yama offers wealth, long life, children and grandchildren, land, music and every pleasure. Nachiketa refuses. These things wear out, he says; keep them. He wants only the answer.

Scene 6

The answer

Satisfied that the boy cannot be bought, Yama teaches. There are two paths, he says: , the good, and , the pleasant. The wise choose the good. Then he teaches about the self, which is not born and does not die, and describes the body as a chariot with the self as its rider.

Interpretations panel

How different traditions read this story

Advaita Vedānta

The self Yama teaches is not different from Brahman. Realising this is liberation.

Viśiṣṭādvaita

The self is real and distinct, yet inseparable from Brahman, as a body is from the one who lives in it.

Dvaita

The self and Brahman are eternally distinct; the teaching points to dependence on, and devotion to, the divine.

A story about courage

Many retellings use it as a lesson in courage and in questioning elders respectfully but firmly.

Why it matters today

The good or the merely pleasant?

We live in an attention economy built on the pleasant: the next video, the next notification. Nachiketa turns down every pleasant thing on offer to hold out for what is good. What would that look like in your week?
